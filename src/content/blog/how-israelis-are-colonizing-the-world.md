## The Big Trip

For decades, tens of thousands of young Israelis have been going to South and Southeast Asia every year. It's a rite of passage for recently discharged soldiers known as the big trip. See, when Jewish Israelis graduate from high school at 18 years old, most of them are required to serve in the occupation forces. And after spending two or three years, many Israelis take months off afterwards to backpack, party, do drugs, and find themselves.

India is one of the most popular destinations. So popular that Israelis have their own backpacking route, the Hummus Trail. "Places such as Dharamkot, Old Manali, Vashisht, and Kasol have nearly half of their population of Israeli nationality." "Street signs in Hebrew, cafes serving fresh falafel and shakshuka." "One town called Kasol has been nicknamed Little Israel."

Institutions like Chabad and others have also set up shop on the trail. "He's one of a number of rabbis sent from Israel, the United States, and Canada to help young Israeli tourists traveling in this land of gods and goddesses to stay in touch with their Jewish roots." An Israeli who runs a Jewish cultural center in one of the villages said, quote, "There's something in India that allows for a wild freedom, like the Wild West. I sense that they can do whatever they want, be that smoking weed, driving without a license, or making noise. This is what they seek here, and many of them do exactly that, moving through communities across the region as though the usual rules don't apply."

"Locals say their livelihoods are under threat from Israelis working on tourist visas and running businesses without permits." "Some Israeli tourists are said to leave rented motorcycles on the roadside when gas runs out." "Of all foreign visitors, only Israelis have managed to build a religious center where they hold parties and celebrations." "This town is completely overrun with Israelis. I'm talking a three to one ratio." "Alongside reports of several businesses illegally run by Israeli citizens." "Smoking weed in hospitals, barring Thais from certain businesses, hotels, and public spaces."

Wait, they've been barring Thai citizens from places in Thailand? Obnoxious tourists with many passports and towns accommodating foreign visitors exist all over the world. But with Israeli tourists, there's something else at play. The disregard for local law, the entitlement, the disrespect. Where does all this come from? To understand the tourist, you have to understand the culture that produced them.

## The Freier

One of the most important words to understand in Israeli society is freier, usually translated as sucker. You see, in Israel, being a freier doesn't just mean you're easily fooled. It's more about missing the chance to cut corners, dodge rules, or get ahead by any means necessary. In a society where bending the rules is often seen as savvy, being a freier means you're the one who plays it straight, and that's not a compliment. In fact, being called a freier is one of the worst insults out there. "Join the back of the line." There doesn't seem to be shame about exploiting someone else. The shame is in allowing yourself to be exploited.

That mentality is so ingrained, it has another name as well: the success method. "So, I have a tiny dog named Lilly and I like to go on errands with her. When I went into my local shoe repair shop, the person there told me that I had to hold her if I wanted to come in. So, the next time I went there, I looked for the person that told me that. When I couldn't find them, I put her on the ground like I wanted." "So what was she doing that's just so Israeli? It's called 'cheat the system', which translates to 'success method', meaning doing what I want until someone tells me otherwise." The assumption is that there's no harm in trying to push a boundary. And if nobody stops you, you've succeeded.

Today, more than 700,000 Israeli settlers live in the West Bank and East Jerusalem. We can see this philosophy clearly in Palestine and the relentless expansion of settlements and in the continuous testing of what the world will tolerate. Pushing these boundaries requires a violence that doesn't remain confined to Palestine. It travels with the settlers.

## Project Beta

There's another place in the world where we can more clearly see how they think. A place that isn't exotic and different, but among people they recognize as their peers. And it's here where a different kind of settler lives. One who is polite and by the book. And who reveals how Zionism sees any land, regardless of the race of who it belongs to.

Nestled beneath the Italian Alps is a region called Valsesia. Known as the greenest valley in Italy, it's a landscape of forested mountains and quiet towns. It's also becoming home to a growing community of Israelis. They're part of an initiative called Progetto Beta, launched by an Israeli-Italian, which has already brought in 80 Israeli families to the region.

The arrangement is presented as a win-win. Valsesia was suffering from demographic decline, and these Israelis were looking for a new place to call home. They're middle-class, educated, very secular, and believe that Israel was once a democracy that is now crumbling. Not because of the genocide, or apartheid settler colonialism, but because this Zionism doesn't fit with their fantasy of what it should be. This group of settlers are what's known as liberal Zionists. And to understand them, we need to understand why they left.

It began in early 2023. "Protesters blocked roads and scuffled with police in Tel Aviv on Wednesday morning, incensed by a controversial plan to overhaul the Israeli judicial system." "I fought in Israel. I fought for 30 years in this country to save this country. Now, I'm fighting against the dictatorship that's going to happen here." "A judicial reform had hundreds of thousands of Israelis out in the streets, mostly secular Jews. They're the largest group in Israel, and are the backbone of its economy." "Unfortunately, many, many of my friends checked the options in other countries. I will not raise my kids in a country which is not democratic." Fearing the collapse of what they believe is Israeli democracy, many began leaving to settle elsewhere. That's when Project Beta began.

This article starts with a quote written by one of the settlers: "As the number of hours of light in their country's democracy keeps diminishing, more and more Israelis are arriving in the mountainous valley in their search for a new start. Underlying all this is a layer of pain, the pain of good Israelis who believed that after 2,000 years they could rest on their laurels, but were now taking up the wanderer's staff once again." Notice how he says good Israelis. The founder of Project Beta uses similar language, saying he wanted to help quote "good Israelis" escape what Israel was becoming.

Inherent in all this is a kind of entitlement. If it's not working out here, I have the right to go and try this project elsewhere. Zionism claims it is a movement of return, and yet all these people who acknowledge how essential they are to the makeup of the colony, feel comfortable abandoning the project to try it elsewhere. The settler mentality is not bound to Palestine. That was just a convenient narrative.

You can hear it in how they talk about the town they've moved to in Italy: "There's something virginal about this place in the sense of innocent, but also open. It wants to be fertilized." Notice that the valley isn't described as an existing home with its own history, which goes back for centuries. And you can hear it in how they talk about Palestine, the land they claim is their homeland: "With all due respect for the talk about the beautiful land of Israel, Israel is perhaps beautiful compared to Syria or Saudi Arabia, but Europe and the Alps are a different world."

This is not surprising when every settler quoted in the article is a settler from somewhere else. To be a settler is to have no relationship with the land. It's just a place to settle, and to feel entitled to it over its indigenous inhabitants takes a certain cocktail of supremacy and entitlement.

For one of the settlers in Italy, a life coach specializing in consciousness studies, the push to leave the colony came in October 2023. She needs quiet to work. And when she realized that she'd have to give courses from her safe room, she decided to leave. "The biggest threat to Israel is religion and it's a threat that cannot be resolved. And when it comes into confrontation with a conflict over land, that's a place I can no longer live in." There's so much to unpack there, but the biggest takeaway is how far removed she is from reality, and from how she is a settler and not a victim.

Where does this victimization come from? "Tens of thousands of Israeli high school students fly to Poland each year to learn firsthand about the Holocaust." "Israeli children are immersed from an early age in stories of exile, persecution, and catastrophe. Holocaust education begins in nursery school and continues all the way until graduation, all with the undertone that there is the constant possibility that history could repeat itself." Fresh from learning all of that, by 18, nearly every Jewish Israeli enters the occupation forces.

"We brought up our children on Zionist ideals because all in all we believed in the state. We all volunteered for a year of community service and served in the IDF.
