If you look at the Arab world today, it can seem like we're at the end of the story. "This was North Gaza. It now resembles the bottom of a fire pit." "Approved the deal, which is worth nearly $35 billion." "Lebanese families were supposed to return to their villages near the border in late January. Their villages are unrecognizable." "...the so-called Abraham Accords in 2020, which normalized relations between Israel and the UAE." With a region that has been struggling for what feels like always, it may seem like fragmentation is somehow inherent to us. But not only is that wrong, it's ahistorical. History shows a vast region, diverse in religion and ethnicity, but connected over centuries by language, trade, and memory. A region that, when it was united, brought great contributions to the world. "Medieval Western Christian mathematics would be inconceivable without what the Arabs transmitted to the West." And the interesting part is that every one of these moments of greatness emerged from times that looked just as dark as this one. "Plunging Islam into the darkest period it had ever yet experienced." "It's not that the Arab world is uniquely doomed to collapse, but that every civilization is." It's known as the cycle of empires, first outlined by the Arab father of sociology, Ibn Khaldun. Civilizations rise through the strength of their asabiya, or their shared purpose, their willingness to sacrifice for something bigger. Then they prosper. But with time, comfort and power erode it, and they become complacent. Then comes corruption, fracture, and eventual collapse. And when they do collapse, there's usually another group, forged stronger in resistance to the old order, ready to take their place. We can see this clearly with three chapters of Arab and Islamic history, where each contributed greatly to the world, but only after rising from the ashes. So, let's rewind to remember what our region can look like when it's united by purpose, not divided by self-preservation. ## The Black Banners: Rise of the Abbasids When people think of a time when the Arabs were powerful, they usually think of the Abbasids. But everything they built emerged from the decay of their predecessor. Before them, the Islamic world was ruled by the Umayyads, a dynasty seated in Damascus that oversaw the expansion of Islamic rule from the bottom of France all the way to India. But 80 years into their rule, the Umayyad rulers were seen as distant, decadent, and unpopular overlords in Damascus. "It seems clear that the small Arab Muslim elite that dominated this great empire was increasingly unpopular with many of its subjects." And in the east, a revolution was brewing. "The symbol of Umayyad authority was their white banner. But in 747, a new symbol rises to challenge their rule: the black banners of the Abbasids." The Abbasids promised a return to moral leadership and greater inclusion of non-Arab Muslims. And in 750, they succeeded in overthrowing the Umayyads and taking power of the Islamic world. It was a magnet for specialists and scholars from far-flung lands, a haven of invention and scholarship. In 762, the caliph al-Mansur ordered the construction of a new capital on the banks of the Tigris: Madinat al-Salam, or what we know as Baghdad. It would be the greatest metropolis of its age and the center of one of the most extraordinary intellectual outpourings in human history. Baghdad's scholars led the world in many fields. "It was a period when science, poetry, philosophy, maths, astronomy and medicine flourished." At the heart of this knowledge production was Bayt al-Hikma, or the House of Wisdom, where Greek, Persian, and Sanskrit texts were being shipped in from across the world, translated into Arabic, debated, corrected, and expanded. In addition to thousands of old Persian texts, the likes of Hippocrates, Galen, Archimedes, Euclid, Plato, and Aristotle were all saved from oblivion. "They also made many of their own original discoveries, knowledge that would come to be much sought after by the medieval West." And the people at the heart of this movement weren't just Muslim. There were Christians, Jews. It was an Arab-led world that absorbed Persians, Africans, Turks, and others into a shared political and cultural order held together by Arabic as its common language. They would go on to rule for over 500 years. But just as Ibn Khaldun's cycle promises, and just like their Umayyad predecessors, that would eventually come apart. In the final centuries of their rule, power began decentralizing to autonomous provinces, and the caliphate was politically overtaken by other emerging powers. The caliph became more of a ceremonial figurehead than a political ruler. Baghdad went from being a thriving, glistening city at the center of the world to a city overcome with sectarian violence and decay. ## Al-Andalus, a Lost Paradise But as the Abbasid world fractured and Baghdad's authority hollowed out, a different experiment in unity was already unfolding far to the west, one forged by a man who had survived his own dynasty's destruction at Abbasid hands. What we know today as Spain and Portugal once had a different name: Al-Andalus, a place that thrived under Muslim rule for 800 years as one of the most prosperous, cultured, and intellectually vibrant places on Earth. They brought with them astronomy, music, philosophy, physics, and mathematics. "A lost paradise, Alfred." But this lost paradise emerged from a time of darkness. The Iberian Peninsula was first conquered by the Umayyads, then the Abbasids, but only shortly. See, when the Abbasids overthrew the Umayyads, they also hunted down and massacred the entire ruling family. But one managed to survive: a young prince who escaped through Egypt and across North Africa, pursued by Abbasid agents the entire time. He kept going until he arrived in Iberia. His name was Abd al-Rahman. In 756, he declared it the Emirate of Cordoba. At the time, the peninsula was embroiled in a civil war and effectively an isolated island, surrounded by the sea on one side and encroaching Christian kingdoms seeking to reclaim it on the other. And yet under the Umayyads, Al-Andalus flourished. "When Abd al-Rahman arrived in Spain, he came here to Cordoba, where the city was in complete disarray. You have to remember that it's in this context that the Arabs arrive, not as marauding destroyers, but sometimes as saviors." The caliphs commissioned huge buildings, masterpieces of architecture. One of them was the Cordoba mosque, and another, the Alhambra in Granada. They introduced new crops, taught farmers how to irrigate more effectively, and reopened ancient trade routes to revitalize the economy. And the Umayyad rulers used this wealth to promote culture and invite brilliant minds in all subjects to their courts. By the 10th century, Cordoba was one of the largest cities in Europe and home to an incredible culture unheard of at the time. Muslims, Christians, and Jews lived under a system that allowed intellectual and cultural exchange. And Al-Andalus became home to libraries that rivaled Baghdad's. The Arabs founded the medical specializations of dentistry, pharmacology, anatomy, and surgery, all of them disciplines that were still to be developed in the West. All practiced their religions freely, and Arabic replaced Latin and became the most commonly spoken language across ethnicities and religions. Al-Andalus was and remains a model for what can come from active, meaningful coexistence. But like Baghdad before it, this unity did not last. "The Christians had always held on to the far north of the country, and now they were gaining ground." Internal rivalry slowly eroded Al-Andalus, fracturing it into dozens of competing city-states, each vying for power. This fragmentation weakened collective defense. Over time, Christian kingdoms to the north advanced southward, conquering cities one by one. When divided Muslim forces came together in 1086, they dealt a crushing blow to the Castilian army and stopped the encroachment for generations. But the unity didn't last. And by 1492, the last Muslim stronghold fell. After the Catholic monarchs took over the city of Granada, they began to destroy all evidence that the Muslims had ever been in Spain. In the following century, the Spanish authorities persecuted and expelled 300,000 Muslims and burned as many as a million Arabic books. This was an astonishing act of ethnic cleansing. It put an end to a civilization which had flourished in Spain for 700 years. Al-Andalus was forged and thrived through unity and cultural exchange, and fell through fragmentation. Shared purpose is strength. We saw that in Baghdad, in Al-Andalus, and next in Jerusalem. ## Jerusalem: Division and Revival In 1099, the crusader army occupied Jerusalem. What followed was a 10-day massacre. "It was very difficult for crusaders to distinguish between local Muslim population, local Jewish population, and local Christian population, because they all looked the same. They all looked like Arabs." Some sources say 100,000 were slaughtered. And yet, the Islamic world had no meaningful unified response. Not because it lacked wealth, soldiers, or cities, but because it was profoundly divided. Remember this. See, at the time, the Abbasids and Baghdad were struggling against the Fatimids in Egypt. Within Egypt, assassinations were common and power kept fluctuating. In Syria, Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, and Mosul were all ruled by competing dynasties. And just look at Jerusalem: before the crusaders arrived, the city had gone through Fatimid, Seljuk, and Artuqid rule. The crusaders would go on to take Jaffa, Haifa, Tiberias, Tripoli. And not only did Muslim rulers not unite to oust them, they sometimes leveraged crusader presence to fight against one another. These losses didn't directly threaten the elites' power, so it didn't mobilize them, but it mobilized the people, and the seeds for a new order were sown. Leaders began to emerge to meet the crusader threat: Imad al-Din Zangi, Nur al-Din, men who saw the need to put aside competing local interests and unite against a common political enemy. And they slowly stitched together Mosul, Aleppo, and Damascus into a single front. "Nur al-Din was very clear: one flag, one nation. It's not the Arabs, it's not the Turks, it is not the Kurds, it's his army, everybody. We're all Muslims. This is not a Palestinian cause. This is not an Arab issue. This is not the issue of... this is a Muslim cause that concerns every single Muslim." Nur al-Din understood that armies alone weren't enough. What was needed was an ideological revival. He drew scholars into Aleppo and Damascus and revived religious institutions around a single radical idea: Muslim unity and the duty to resist to save Jerusalem. What was missing was Egypt: wealthy, strategic, and essential to winning the war. Though still ruled by the Fatimids, it was weakened by decline and infighting. Nur al-Din sent an army to Egypt that expelled the crusaders from the Nile Valley, under the command of one of his men, a Kurdish general called Shirkuh. He took with him a reluctant young man who would write his name through the history of the crusades. His name was Salah al-Din al-Ayyubi, also known as Saladin. He would declare Egypt loyal to the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad, reuniting Egypt with Syria at last. This ended the two-century-old Fatimid caliphate and marked the beginning of the new Ayyubid dynasty, a state now large and strong enough to carry out Nur al-Din's grand plan to expel the crusaders. He didn't march straight for Jerusalem. Instead, he began putting the house in order from the new capital of Damascus. He stabilized administration, subdued rival princes, unified Egypt, Syria, and much of the Levant, and focused on Nur al-Din's core mission: reviving Islamic education and a shared purpose. Saladin himself embodied this humble, grounded ethic. He lived simply, wore plain clothes, and would later die with little personal wealth, most of it having been distributed through charity and public works. Under the Ayyubids, Damascus became the heart of a unified Muslim realm and the center for Islamic revival. Scholars were shaping a population that believed unity was possible again. Sixteen years after founding the Ayyubid dynasty, and 88 years since its occupation, Saladin marched to retake Jerusalem, and won. And the contrast could not have been sharper. There was no massacre. The crusaders were allowed to leave peacefully. And for those that wished to leave but couldn't pay the exit tax, Saladin personally paid it on their behalf. After its liberation and under the Ayyubids, Jerusalem was reintegrated into the Islamic world as a Muslim-governed, Arab-inhabited, multi-religious city. The city remained politically stable under the Ayyubids for another 80 years. But things started to come apart when Saladin died. He had no clear successor, and different family members ended up ruling different territories and competing for power. This opened the door once again for the crusaders to take advantage. The three Ayyubid brothers were engaged in deep infighting, and one of them, al-Kamil, ruler of Egypt, took an infamous decision. "In 1229, the big blow: the loss of Jerusalem, not even by military defeat, but by Saladin's nephew signing a treaty that handed it back to the crusaders for his own political preservation." "What had built required unity, restraint, and shared purpose. Without them, as it always does, the cycle turned." By the mid-13th century, the Ayyubids were finished. A new Muslim force, the Mamluks, seized Egypt, then Syria, ending Ayyubid rule altogether. They would be the ones to eventually expel the crusaders completely, only to be later brought down themselves by the Ottomans. And on the cycle goes. ## What Ibn Khaldun Saw Khaldun didn't come up with this theory in a vacuum. He was born just a few decades after one of the greatest intellectual centers in human history, the House of Wisdom, was burned to the ground. To Ibn Khaldun, the Abbasid world wasn't a golden age. It was a fresh ruin. He lived through one of the most turbulent centuries and witnessed firsthand the birth of new states, the death throes of Al-Andalus, and the advance of the Christian reconquest, the Hundred Years' War, the expansion of the Ottoman Empire, and the great epidemic of the Black Death. And it's from these difficult times that he was able to see that every collapse carries seeds for renewal if what follows is shared purpose. At a time when our region feels overcome with tragedy and disunity, we need to remember that this is just a small chapter in a long cyclical history. The same force that built Baghdad, created Al-Andalus, and recaptured Jerusalem is sprouting somewhere today. A golden age doesn't have to be a memory. It can be a future.
