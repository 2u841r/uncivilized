The sentiment of hate and superiority against Arabs and Muslims traces its roots back to the Middle Ages, because that's where our story begins. ## The Arab Empire The Arab Empire began in 633 and expanded across the remnants of the Roman Empire into North Africa, Spain, and eventually up to France. Sound familiar? ## The Crusades This brings me to the most infamous speech of the Middle Ages: Pope Urban II at the Council of Clermont in 1095. He described the Saracens as "an accursed race, utterly alienated from God," invaders who "devastated the land with sword, pillage, and fire," and "defiled the altars with their uncleanliness." He calls them heathens and wicked, accusing them of torturing Christians in horrific ways. Notice how the Arab Muslims are described. Killing them became not only permitted, but sacred. His words launched the Crusades, a series of holy wars by Western European Christians between the 11th and 13th centuries aimed at conquering Jerusalem and the surrounding lands from Arab rule. For nearly 200 years, armies from France, Germany, and England marched into the Middle East, driven by promises of salvation and glory. Even after the crusader states were long gone, their myths and resentments never truly faded and are still glorified to this day. But for the Arabs at that time, they were a horrific murderous campaign. Every accusation lobbed at Arabs and Muslims was actually a confession by Europeans about their own behavior. "From the Arab Muslim historical accounts, as far as we can tell, this is not a theological clash." "The Muslims who are there are like, who are these crazy Franks?" As historian John Tolan argues, anti-Arab and anti-Muslim narratives were never just prejudice. They were tools used to authorize and justify military action, legal segregation, and repression. A propaganda story, a myth engineered to unify Europe against a common enemy. But the medieval clash didn't end there. Alongside the failure of the Crusades came another failure in the 14th century: missionary campaigns to convert Muslims. Missionaries concluded that the problem was with the people they were trying to convert. Muslims, like the Jews before them, were portrayed as too stubborn, too sensual, too obsessed with the literal world to grasp any spiritual truth. The Arabs were cast as irrational, childlike, intellectually lazy, and incapable of reason. Creatures who could not be persuaded, so they must be compelled. The logic was chilling. This idea reinforced the superiority of the western mind over the backward, uncivilized oriental. A narrative way too familiar to colonized people today that traces its legacy back to the 1300s. Just as propaganda from medieval Europe justified the Crusades, orientalism in the 18th, 19th, and 20th century justified colonialism and the exploitation of people, land, and resources. Orientalism described how the West saw the east as inferior, uncivilized, and backwards. This was more about defining Europe as the Orient's opposite: superior, civilized, and advanced. Orientalism was the knowledge that authorizes and justifies the West's domination and subjugation of the East. From the French mission to civilize to the British manuals on the government of subject races, the bridge between the Middle Ages and colonialism was built with the same bricks. John Tolan describes what came in the Middle Ages as adolescent orientalism that was at once immature and timeless, that later matured within the context of modern Europe. One of the reasons he cites is that western authors writing about Muslims, Arabs, Turks, or the oriental from the 14th century to the 20th referred to the fundamental texts and images created during medieval Europe. "It develops a kind of image of the timeless orient, as if the orient, unlike the west, doesn't develop. It stays the same. And that's one of the problems with orientalism: it creates an image outside of history of something that is placid and still and eternal, which is simply contradicted by the facts of history. So that in one sense, it's a creation of, you might say, an ideal other." The understanding of the other never evolved, and it was purposefully so. The Arab and Muslim became frozen in time.
