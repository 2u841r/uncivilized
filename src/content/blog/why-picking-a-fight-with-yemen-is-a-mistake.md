Yemen, the land of warriors. Why is a country ravaged by war and an imposed humanitarian crisis defying global superpowers? "New air strikes against Houthi targets in Yemen carried out by the US and the UK." "When you say working, are they stopping the Houthis?" "No." "Are they going to continue?" "Yes." To support Palestine through military action when no one else is. What if I told you that a dance can explain the history of this nation and shed light on their culture of defiance and solidarity? But first, let's go back in time. ## A History of Defiance Because of its geostrategic location and its importance to commerce and trade, empires and great powers coveted Yemen. But no outside force has ever been able to conquer and control the entirety of the country, and trust me, many have tried. In 2524 BC, at the height of its empire, the Romans sent an expedition to take over what was known back then as Arabia Felix. They were met with harsh desert conditions and fierce resistance by local tribes. The great power of the time suffered heavy losses and left in defeat. This is a lesson no one seems to learn, including today's powers. From the Ottomans to the British, every foreign ruler was met with rebellions and revolts from the Yemenis and eventually always driven out. "British High Commissioner Sir Humphrey Trevelyan was leaving this troubled land for good. South Arabia was about to be transformed into the independent People's Republic of South Yemen. The Union Jack was dipped for the last time on these inhospitable shores. The National Liberation Front geared themselves to take over." In 2014, the Houthis, a group of predominantly Zaidi Shias from the north, deposed the government in Sanaa and took over after years of opposition. But regional powers, some backed by the West, responded by unleashing a devastating war in Yemen. The war and the humanitarian crisis it caused killed hundreds of thousands of people. Yet 10 years on, as history has shown us repeatedly, no external power can crush and subdue this entire nation. ## The Bara And nothing captures the spirit of defiance more than the Bara, the Yemeni tribal dance of war. To the fast speed of drums, men perform their steps and moves in unison while brandishing their weapons, most traditionally the jambia, a dagger that is a symbol of honor and the Yemeni warrior culture. The ancient origins of the Bara come from battle, often performed to signal the invasion of an adversarial tribe and was meant to prepare warriors to fight. It is the OG hype dance. The Bara today is at the heart of Yemeni culture, performed at weddings, important events, and for guests of honor. But the dance tells us something else about Yemen: the importance of tribes in society. Tribes are the foundation of communities and define most social relations within much of the country. Depending on the region, each tribe has its own identity, customs, and by extension its own version of the Bara. This reflects the beautiful diversity of Yemen, but as these identities intersect with religion, sectarianism, and politics, they also have produced differences that have fueled divisions and embroiled the country in decades of infighting. Despite this, there seems to be one thing most Yemenis can agree on: their love for Palestine. And as history has shown us, Yemenis never shy away from a fight. Rooted in their shared Arab and Muslim identity, Yemeni solidarity with Palestine also comes from the justice of the Palestinian cause as a fight against imperialism that has for centuries also played out. ## Hitting the Global Economy As Israel's war on Gaza raged on, the Houthis took action and many in the country rallied behind them. Despite their differences, they decided to hit the global economy. See, Yemen sits on one of the main arteries of global trade, the Bab al-Mandab strait of the Red Sea. 12 to 15% of the world trade passes through this route, and when it is closed, it can destabilize the world. Normally, a trip from the Gulf to the UK would take 14 days, but when this route is closed, it takes ships 24 days to travel around the southern tip of Africa. Knowing very well the economic impact, the Houthis targeted ships headed to Israel through Bab al-Mandab. The military operations aimed at squeezing and isolating the Israeli economy, which was already under a lot of pressure. As a consequence, shipping prices rose and companies avoided traveling through the Red Sea for fear of the Houthis. And so Yemen, a country deeply impoverished by war, brought the world economy and the West to its knees. Undeterred by threats from superpowers, the Houthis continued. From these ships, they broadcasted to the world this culture of defiance, and as you might have guessed, they were dancing the Bara, the Yemeni dance of war. Those images captured the hearts and minds of the world because no other people or country was willing to militarily intervene for Palestine as Israel was conducting its genocide. And when Yemen was bombed by the US and the UK for their military operations, millions of Yemenis went to the streets and chanted: "We don't care, bring it on, let's make it a World War." There's no fight Yemenis are willing to back down from, and they understand that war is the extension of politics by other means, whether it is deploying military operations as a form of economic sanctions, or showing Western hypocrisy that is willing to act and bomb for cargo ships but not to stop a genocide. If the Bara and history can teach us anything, it is that you can never subdue or crush the Yemeni spirit of defiance. These powers are picking a fight with the wrong people, because Yemen is a land of warriors.
