"Jesus of Nazareth was a radical revolutionary." Was Jesus a revolutionary? For many, that's a controversial question to ask. It touches at the foundation of people's religion and faith. But this isn't just a matter of intellectual debate; this belief has fueled revolutions and revolutionaries that have shaped the world, from Latin America to the United States, and from the Middle East to Africa. It's even influenced the current pope. So where did this idea come from, and how did it take hold? For that, we need to understand Jesus in a way you probably never have before. ## Three Jesuses "There are three kinds of Jesuses. The first kind of Jesus we can call the historical Jesus; that's the kind of Jesus that historians can pretty much verify using their tools. The second Jesus is the Christ of faith; the Christ of faith we might say is the Christ we find in our Bibles, in our New Testament. And then the third Jesus is the real Jesus, the actual person who walked and lived in that time and area." The Christ of faith is the one you're most familiar with through the Bible: he was a peaceful preacher who performed miracles and told you to turn the other cheek. His main teachings were not about this world, but salvation for the afterlife and the kingdom of heaven. But for people who tout Jesus's revolutionary credentials, this couldn't be further from the reality. "Jesus the man, instead of Jesus the Christ: the historical person who walked the Earth two thousand years ago and said these things, and what the context of what he said meant at the time, and how we should read that today." They are more concerned with the historical Jesus, and to understand him, we need to look back to first-century Palestine. And the story goes like this. ## The Historical Jesus Jesus was a Jew living in Palestine under Roman occupation. He was a peasant from the Galilee that became this prolific preacher, creating a mass movement of followers. His disciples were of the poor, the marginalized, and the forgotten within society. They saw him as the Messiah. According to this narrative, which was made mainstream by Reza Aslan, Jesus was calling for the establishment of the kingdom of God. But this wasn't a reference to the afterlife or some heavenly world to come; this was a call for revolution. "Jesus the Messiah has no concerns for the cares of this world whatsoever. His only concern is, you know, the world to come." "Jesus of Nazareth was a radical revolutionary whose sole focus was on the suffering of the people in this world right now, and the power structures that were creating that suffering." Historical Jesus wanted to overthrow the Roman Empire in Palestine. While he never directly called for violence, Jesus understood that the reality could never be achieved without the use of force. And how did Rome deal with those who challenged the rule of Caesar and the Roman Empire? Crucifixion. Historically, that was the punishment Romans carried out against people who committed insurrection, treason, or sedition. For the Romans, calling for the kingdom of God was seen as trying to stoke a rebellion. They saw Jesus as a rebel leading a movement, so they crucified him and wrote "the king of the Jews" on top of his cross. "And a sign was placed above his head that read: this is the king of the Jews." For historians and scholars, Jesus was shaped by his environment and not exceptional to it. First-century Palestine saw the rise of many messianic figures and movements. They believed in the rule of God, not the rule of man, and on that basis opposed the Roman Empire. These movements prayed for divine intervention in their fight against Rome, and according to historical records, this time period was characterized by many different uprisings against the Roman Empire. They were often crushed and their leaders crucified. It is in this context that Jesus emerged. Like others of his time who wanted to overthrow the Romans, his teachings about the kingdom of God, where the poor and the weak would replace the rich and the powerful, can be understood as seeking to challenge the established order. They weren't spiritual; they were political. And in that sense, Jesus was a revolutionary. While this theory is not widely accepted by some scholars, and is controversial for those who hold onto the Christ of faith, it does give us a sense of how Jesus is seen as a revolutionary. ## Liberation Theology There's also another view, one with its origins in Latin America, that has taken Jesus's teachings and used them to inspire many around the world to rise up against injustice. "I believe that in liberation theology, of solidarity with others, particularly with those most poor, it is also clear the poor are loved preferentially by God, because they live in a condition that is contrary to his will. And this should be our attitude." Emerging in the 1960s and '70s, during a time characterized by dictatorships, repression, poverty, and social inequality in Latin America, liberation theology was a movement that sought to take on injustices and transform society. As a theology, it was rooted in the Catholic Church and was built on Jesus's embrace of the poor and marginalized. Its intellectual foundations come from scriptures, historical context, and the application of Marxist frameworks. "It seems to me that to announce the kingdom of God is to announce the kingdom of life. Abundant life: all of life, personal, social, spiritual, material. This is announcing the kingdom of God." Liberation theologians saw Jesus and his teachings as revolutionary for their time, and the source of liberation of people from their oppressive material conditions. They preached and practiced faith as action. "So we organized meetings and planned our strategy. With the help of the church, we got a human rights lawyer to take the government to court to challenge them on their right to relocate us." Believing that Christianity required active participation in the struggle for justice, to commemorate their struggles and celebrate their victories, the women of the base community write songs. This song is about their fight to keep their homes. Liberation theology also moved beyond the concept of personal sin and highlighted the concept of structural sin: like systems that perpetuate violence and suffering. Some regard it as a response to the Christianity used to justify imperialism, slavery, and colonialism. "What the Christian slaveholders did with their Bibles was to take Paul's descriptions of the New Testament world, 'slaves, obey your masters', which was descriptive of the state of life, and to take that as prescriptive: therefore masters may have slaves, and slaves must obey in all situations. So this was a type of literal interpretation, and they could claim that they were being faithful to scripture, that they were interpreting scripture literally." Liberation theology was not a monolith, and so theologians were in disagreement about armed struggle as a means for liberation. Gustavo Gutiérrez, one of the founding fathers of liberation theology, didn't openly advocate for violence but understood the role resistance played in the context of injustice. While on the other hand, Camilo Torres Restrepo, a Catholic priest, engaged in the armed struggle as part of the guerrilla movement in Colombia. He said the duty of every Christian is to be a revolutionary. Liberation theology shaped struggles all over Latin America, from Mexico to Colombia, from indigenous movements to workers' movements and beyond. Some were even assassinated for promoting it, like Oscar Romero in El Salvador. "The US Army helped defeat liberation theology, and that's true. There was a war between the US military and the Latin American Catholic Church, which had adopted a preferential option for the poor and was working to help poor people. So they had to be slaughtered." But it couldn't be contained, and spread around the world, inspiring revolutionaries and revolutions all over. In the US, there was Reverend Martin Luther King. "Mine eyes have seen the glory of the coming of the Lord." In South Africa, there was Desmond Tutu and the anti-apartheid movement. "Our struggle is going to succeed because it is a just struggle." In Palestine, we have Palestinian liberation theology, in the face of Israeli settler colonialism and Christian Zionism. "In Palestine we speak of the empire. Here we confront the theology of empire: a disguise for superiority, supremacy, chosenness, and entitlement." And in the Vatican, there's Pope Francis. ## The Debate Historically, the Catholic Church wasn't too receptive to liberation theology, critiquing its closeness to Marxism and for connecting faith to political ideologies and struggles. And for many Christians, they just simply disagreed with that interpretation of Jesus. "You ain't seen the insurrection yet! You keep on pushing our buttons, you low-down sorry compromisers, you God-hating communist America, you'll find out what an insurrection is, because we ain't playing your garbage, we ain't playing your mess. My Bible says that the church of the living God is an institution that the gates of hell shall not prevail against it, and the Bible says that we'll take it by force." Regardless of the debate, what has been clear is the impact of liberation theology on shaping the world for the last half century. And at the center of it is one core belief: Jesus was a revolutionary and liberator. So, was Jesus a revolutionary? Historical Jesus, maybe he was; we will never fully know. But there's another question: can Christianity inspire revolutionary ideas and actions to take on injustice and systems of domination everywhere? If the answer is yes, then I hope he was a revolutionary, and will continue to be.
