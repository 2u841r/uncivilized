## Loud and Proud Iraqi

"Before I even started, I remember lots of people telling me, as the strap line, don't call it Iraqi cuisine, just say Middle Eastern food or Middle East." "That's an interesting point. How come?" "I was just like, safe, you know? Safe. Iraq has its own connotations when you mention that word. And I was just like, no, this is loud and proud. The cuisine and the flavor was so unique, and I didn't want to go down the route that everyone kind of knew. I wanted to separate and differentiate myself, because the cuisine and the identity deserve that."

"A hundred percent. I was with a Palestinian chef in New York talking about Palestinian food there and how it's being appropriated. In the early '90s they were a Middle Eastern restaurant, and then they became an Arab restaurant, and now they're a Palestinian restaurant. ## Between Two Worlds

"English, Irish mother. Born and bred London, you know. Grew up not speaking Arabic, my Iraqiness wasn't as present. I never saw myself as Iraqi growing up. I think I shied away from it. I didn't have a real understanding of my roots, because every time I would approach and talk to my dad about his upbringing and his past, and obviously everything that's gone on, he wouldn't really have time for me. It would be too traumatic for him. I couldn't get anything out, couldn't get any info. So then back to square one."

"That tends to happen a lot actually with the older generation who left their homelands to go elsewhere. A lot of their memories of back home are very trauma-inducing and triggering, and a lot of them don't talk about it, they keep it in. Which is interesting to see in an Iraqi context, because I've experienced it in the Palestinian context."

"Years ago tonight, British forces joining the US-led ground invasion of Iraq." What was it like for you, as a British Iraqi, seeing the country you're in also be part of an invasion? "It was complicated, it was complex, it was triggering, it was sad, quite alienating as well and isolating. And obviously then, at the time, I'm seeing on the news the depiction of Iraq is X, and then it's like, hold on, my community and what I'm witnessing isn't what the TV is representing. It was a big impetus to why Juma is here, because it was like, well, no: life, my pals, and people around me think that Iraq is that? No, wait, I need to do something about that. And obviously food was, it always is across all countries and cultures, a great vehicle to show a different side. So food was the identity vehicle." "It's still on the Sundays."
